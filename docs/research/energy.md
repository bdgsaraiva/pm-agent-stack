# Energy specialist: literature review and design (S1)

Written 24 Oct 2026 for phase S1 (`docs/project/specialists-kickoff.md`). There were three research passes, run as parallel sub-agents:
- oil economics, storage theory and curves;
- price reactions to scheduled reports and surprise measurement;
- an edge-source check.

Every row below was opened on the page it cites. When the publisher was paywalled, the source is the abstract page plus an author or working-paper PDF, and the row says "WP" where a number comes from the working-paper version. Anything not confirmed on an opened page is marked *not page-checked*. The design rules for the whole pillar are in `docs/research/specialists-architecture.md`.

## 1. Papers: finding → what it changes

### Oil economics, storage and curves
| Finding | What it changes | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Theory of storage.** F − S = S·r + w − c. The convenience yield falls "at a decreasing rate as aggregate inventory rises" (Kaldor 1939; Working 1949; Brennan 1958, as summarised in GHR's WP p.6; the originals were blocked by JSTOR). | The card states curve shape as an inventory state through this identity, never as a price forecast. | Kaldor (1939) RES 7(1); Working (1949) AER 39(6); Brennan (1958) AER 48(1); summary in https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w13249/w13249.pdf |
| **Pindyck (2001).** Marginal convenience yield ψ = (1+r)P − F + k. Volatility raises storage demand, and with it the convenience yield. Weak backwardation is the usual state. | A second curve driver besides stock levels: volatility. Computed in code, never from narrative. | https://ceepr.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/2001-002.pdf (eq. 6) |
| **Fama & French (1987).** The basis varies with interest rates and seasonal convenience yields. Futures show forecast power for 10 of 21 commodities. | The basis is storage-driven; its forecast power is weak and commodity-specific. Describe it, don't forecast with it. | https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jnlbus/v60y1987i1p55-73.html (abstract) |
| **Gorton, Hayashi & Rouwenhorst (2013).** The basis is a decreasing, non-linear function of inventories. Slope at I/I* = 1: crude −303.9 %/yr (t −6.06, R² 0.46), heating oil −137.6, natural gas −174.9 (WP Table 3). For energy, the 1.0 vs 0.75 slopes do not differ significantly. Low-inventory portfolios beat high ones by 8.06 %/yr (t 3.19) in the cross-section, but crude's time-series link is not significant (t −0.60). | The main literature size for inventory vs basis (drivers `inventory-vs-basis*`). Use a near-linear local slope for energy. Low stocks explain carry; they are not a timing signal. | https://www.nber.org/papers/w13249 (WP) |
| **Kilian (2009).** Shocks differ: precautionary demand gives an "immediate, persistent, and large" rise; aggregate demand a "delayed, but sustained" rise; supply disruptions a "small and transitory" rise. | An explanation names the shock type, as inference (driver `shock-type-attribution`). | https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.99.3.1053 |
| **Kilian & Murphy (2014).** Inventories identify speculative demand. Impact supply elasticity ≤ 0.025. Demand elasticity in use −0.26 (68% band −0.54 to −0.09) (WP). | Unexplained builds alongside a rising price are the signature of speculative or precautionary demand. Elasticity size filed with its band. | https://www.frbsf.org/Kilian_Role-of-Inventories-and-Speculative-Trading.pdf (WP) |
| **Baumeister & Hamilton (2019).** Supply elasticity 0.16, demand −0.35 (WP posterior medians). Supply matters more and speculation less than in earlier work. | The central elasticity pair. Never attribute a move to speculation by default. | https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20151569; WP https://www.socialpolitik.de/sites/default/files/ausschuss/makrooekonomie/update_09_11_2017/DP2017-03_BaumeisterHamilton.pdf |
| **Counterpoint:** Baumeister & Hamilton (2020) vs Kilian & Zhou (2019). A recursive identification implies a supply elasticity of exactly zero. Re-running KM14's code with another random seed changes the conclusions. | Supply/demand attributions are model-dependent: show a range, labelled as inference. | https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jhamilto/BH4.pdf |
| **Caldara, Cavallo & Iacoviello (2019).** IV estimates: supply 0.081, demand −0.080. Supply and demand shocks explain 36.5% and 37% of 2-year price variance; with supply elasticity set to 0, the supply share falls to 2%. | A third, middle estimate. Shows how much small elasticity choices change the attribution. | https://www.matteoiacoviello.com/research_files/CCI.pdf |
| **Hamilton (2009).** A 5.5 mb/d demand shift against flat supply takes $55 to $142 at elasticity 0.06, or to $97 at 0.10. | A balance gap's price effect is very sensitive to the elasticity. Never give one point. | https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w15002/w15002.pdf (WP p.13) |
| **Baumeister & Kilian (2016).** Oil prices stay hard to predict, and expectations differ across groups. | Explain, don't forecast. Say whose expectation a "surprise" is measured against. | https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.30.1.139 (abstract) |
| **Känzig (2021).** OPEC supply news that lifts price 10% lowers production 0.7% and raises inventories 1.2% at the peak (MPRA WP). | On OPEC days, a price rise with stocks building is consistent with supply news (driver `opec-supply-news`). | https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/106249/1/MPRA_paper_106249.pdf |
| **Alquist & Kilian (2010).** A no-change forecast beats futures for crude (1991–2007). The spread reflects precautionary demand. | The curve is not an expected path; no-change is the benchmark. | https://crei.cat/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/killian.pdf (WP) |
| **Hamilton & Wu (2014).** Crude futures risk premia changed after 2005 (8-week contract 2.9% discount 1990–2004, smaller and sometimes negative after). | Part of the basis is a time-varying risk premium. Flag the 2005 break. | https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19056/w19056.pdf |
| **Kilian & Lee (2014).** Speculative premium of $5–14 in mid-2008, $3–13 in Libya 2011, $0–9 in Iran 2012. | Episode premia are ranges that depend on the inventory proxy. | https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jimfin/v42y2014icp71-87.html (abstract) |
| **Büyükşahin, Lee, Moser & Robe (2013).** Brent–WTI follows Cushing bottlenecks, the cycle and the WTI slope. The Cushing level is not significant. | Use the Cushing fill ratio and the prompt slope (driver `brent-wti-cushing`; the capacity series is an open question). | https://www.eia.gov/finance/markets/reports_presentations/2012PaperBrentWTI.pdf (WP) |
| **Ready (2018).** A daily demand/supply split from oil-producer equity returns orthogonal to VIX. | A candidate code classifier for "why did it move", kept as inference. | https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/revfin/v22y2018i1p155-176..html (abstract) |

### Report reactions and surprise measurement
| Finding | What it changes | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Miao, Ramchander, Wang & Yang (2018).** EIA crude surprise vs the Bloomberg median, 446 releases (2003–2011): CL1 −0.482% per sd (t −3.77), adj. R² 0.022. The lead (day −1) is significant (−0.289). There is no drift after. The survey's sd is 1,623 kb against the actual change's 3,475 kb. | Daily close-to-close detects the effect but R² is tiny, so sign agreement and conditional moves are the headline. Measure day −1. A survey removes only about a quarter of the variance (derived). | Author PDF https://api.mountainscholar.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/9938aa1b-96a3-466b-ac76-7710737b5b72/content |
| **Halova, Kurov & Kucher (2014).** OLS response coefficients are attenuated by noisy surprises: true effects are about 2× OLS for petroleum and 4× for gas. | Slopes against a free proxy are **lower bounds**. Never compare them with survey-based slopes. | https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jfutmk/v34y2014i10p911-933.html (abstract; body coefficients *not page-checked*) |
| **Linn, Ederington, Fernando & Guernsey (EIA workshop, 2017).** Own-surprise response per sd: oil −0.392, gasoline −0.484, distillate −0.333, gas −1.005 (intraday). Petroleum weakened after June 2006 (oil −0.429 → −0.271) while gas rose. Cross-product surprises matter. No reaction on the following two days. | Era splits (2006, 2015) and a "recent block" base rate. A cross-product matrix is an open question. Gas reacts more per sd. | https://www.eia.gov/finance/markets/reports_presentations/2017/linn.pdf |
| **Farhangdoost & Etienne (2020).** Gas reactions are larger in the injection season and at high inventory, and "decreased significantly or remained the same over time". | Split gas by season and inventory regime; estimate on rolling windows. | https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/304476/files/18822.pdf |
| **Chiou-Wei, Linn & Zhu (2014).** Gas moves inversely with storage surprises, only on the release day. The response grew after 2005. | Day-0 window for NG. Trends differ by commodity: let the data decide. | https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jimfin/v42y2014icp156-173.html |
| **Linn & Zhu (2004).** Release-interval gas volatility is about 6× other days. A naive (last week's change) expectation is an accepted proxy when no survey exists. | The variance-ratio test needs no survey. Naive and seasonal proxies are legitimate when labelled as such. | https://ou.edu/content/dam/price/Management/Energy%20Institute/docs/Linn%20and%20Zhu%20JFM%202004.pdf |
| **Gay, Simkins & Turac (2009); Ederington et al. (2019).** Analysts add information beyond statistical models. Gas forecasts absorb the time-series information; crude forecasts do not. Storage surprises partly reverse the next week. | A seasonal-norm proxy is noisier than true expectations, so label it. For crude, a code forecast can carry signal; test the 2-week cumulative surprise later. | https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jfutmk/v29y2009i5p451-477.html ; https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/enejou/v40y2019i5p121-142.html |
| **Bu (2014); Chebbi (2019).** The unexpected crude shock lowers release-day returns, but crude's daily conditional variance does not respond (gas volatility does). The effect vanishes in steep sell-offs (Bu). Chebbi finds asymmetry on 2010–13. | Test mean and variance separately. Don't drop CL if its variance ratio is near 1. Regime splits are open questions. | https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eneeco/v46y2014icp485-494.html ; https://ideas.repec.org/a/ids/ijgeni/v42y2019i1-2p1-20.html |
| **Mu (2007).** Gas volatility is higher on Mondays and storage-report days. Weather moves mean and volatility. | Exclude Mondays from gas control days. Weather is a future control. | https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/en/publications/weather-storage-and-natural-gas-price-dynamics-fundamentals-and-v/ |
| **Bjursell, Gentle & Wang (2015).** Large jumps cluster on EIA announcement dates. | Report the big-move rate on release vs other days (the "lift" column). | https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eneeco/v48y2015icp336-349.html |
| **Ye & Karali (2015).** The API report comes Tuesday 16:30 ET, after the settle. EIA's crude response is about twice API's. 11 of 50 EIA reports gave intraday jumps (indicative: 50 weeks). | API news lands in Wednesday's close-to-close return. A daily study cannot separate the two. | https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/205595/files/AAEA_Ye_Karali-2015.pdf |
| **Alturki & Kurov (2022).** A combination forecast proxies expectations, and sequential announcements show inefficiency. | A combination proxy fitted out of sample is an open question. | https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jfutmk/v42y2022i1p172-188.html (abstract) |
| **Kilian & Vega (2011).** No compelling daily response of energy to US macro surprises. | Inventory news is one of the few releases visible at daily frequency. Macro days are not a major confounder. | https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v93y2011i2p660-671.html |
| **EIA WPSR and WNGSR methodology.** Week Friday 07:01 to Friday 07:00. Tables at 10:30 ET Wednesday; holiday weeks move one day (alternates mostly Thursday 12:00 ET). Weekly data are "generally not revised". Gas: Thursday 10:30 ET, revisions above 500 MMcf, reclassifications of 4 Bcf or more, EIA's 5-year average by daily interpolation, sampling error about 2 Bcf. | Release dates by rule (holiday shifts approximate, flagged). Use first-release values where they differ (the issue archive since 2011 is an open item). Gas surprises under about 4 Bcf are noise. | https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/supply/weekly/pdf/appendixb.pdf ; https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/supply/weekly/schedule.php ; https://ir.eia.gov/ngs/methodology.html |

**Searched, not found:** no peer-reviewed paper tests "change vs the 5-year average change" as a proxy for WPSR or WNGSR expectations. Its quality has to be measured on our own data (open question).

## 3. Where sources disagree, and the resolution
1. **Supply elasticity, near zero or 0.08–0.16:** show the range across identifications, labelled as inference. CCI and BH are the central case, KM the low bound.
2. **Basis: convenience yield or risk premium:** describe the curve as an inventory state, with one caveat about the post-2005 risk premium.
3. **Petroleum reaction weakened, gas strengthened** (Linn et al. 2017; Chiou-Wei 2014), while Farhangdoost & Etienne find gas shrinking later: rolling eras per commodity. The table shows since-2015, and the recent block becomes the base rate when it is under half the full sample.
4. **Asymmetry:** none (Miao; Linn) vs pronounced (Chebbi 2010–13). Tested in our data later, never assumed.
5. **Daily crude volatility:** no response (Bu; Chebbi) vs jumps (Bjursell, intraday). Our daily vol ratio for WTI is about 1.05, consistent with "small at daily frequency"; the mean effect is reported regardless.

## 4. Design changes vs the brief's spec
- **Surprise definition = the fact pack's** (same and adjacent ISO weeks of the prior five years, in sd of those changes), so a playbook and a fact pack never show two numbers for one week. The theme-B agent suggested scaling by the proxy's own trailing sd; consistency wins, and the proxy is labelled.
- **Headline statistics:** sign agreement, the big-move lift and conditional moves. The slope is secondary and a lower bound.
- **Day −1 is reported** because API and pre-positioning show up there. Our WTI data agree: day −1 slope −0.18 per sd (t −3.3).
- **No scheduled card.** Bernardo, 24 Oct: ad hoc `/ask` only until the orchestrator (S6).
- **Sources added** (Bernardo, 24 Oct: all four that passed): ANP Brazil, EIA STEO vintages, JODI snapshots (vintages filed in the facts log from now on) and EIA monthly rigs. Also the natural gas storage series and the 12-month curve collection (Yahoo, personal use), so the S2 carry module inherits a history. OPEC MOMR (Cloudflare), Baker Hughes weekly (blocks bots) and CME settlements (terms ban scraping) fail.
- **Recency rule** and **era splits** (2006, 2015) added from theme B.
- **STEO availability** = the sheet's forecast date + 7 days (the sheet date is when the forecast was completed; EIA publishes it later). Review finding, 24 Oct.
